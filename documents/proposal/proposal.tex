\section{Problem Statement and User Need}
\label{sec:problem}

As people age, day-to-day tasks that were once routine become increasingly difficult, yet many older adults choose to live independently despite this. An elderly person living alone who falls has no reliable way of summoning help, and caregivers or family members have no way of knowing something happened until it's too late.

This gap affects three primary groups directly: the elderly individual living alone, who bears the immediate risk of a fall, the caregiver or family member directly responsible for their well-being, and the care facility that needs to keep constant track of its patients. Table~\ref{tab:needs} lists the needs of every stakeholder:

\input{table1_stakeholder_needs}

Taken together, these needs point to one measurable target for the system as a whole, stated below as the project objective:\\

\textbf{Objective --} Missed falls are prioritized, as they are the most serious failure mode: detect at least $95\%$ of real falls (miss rate $\leq 5\%$) and deliver a confirmed alert to the caregiver/family and care facility within 25 seconds.

Two stakeholders behind this diagram want different things from the same design decision. The wearer's success criterion is retaining the ability to cancel a false trigger before anyone is bothered (N2); the caregiver's is being notified the instant a fall occurs, with no delay standing between a real emergency and their response (N4). These two criteria collide directly: any nonzero cancel window granted to the wearer is time subtracted from how quickly the caregiver can be alerted to a genuine fall. No single window length fully satisfies both, and resolving this trade-off is one of the provisional requirements addressed in Section~\ref{sec:requirements}.

\section{Platform and Custom Hardware}
\label{sec:platform}

The watch imposes several fixed limits on this design. Table~\ref{tab:platform} lists only the specifications this project actually touches, each tagged with its source and marked as a constraint.

\input{table3_platform}

The custom hardware is a waist-worn protoboard carrying its own microcontroller with an integrated BLE radio, a 6-axis IMU, and a barometric pressure sensor powered independently of the watch. Since it is worn separately rather than physically attached, it cannot reach the watch's I2C, UART, or USB expansion pads; instead, the two devices exchange fall-event and validation data wirelessly over Bluetooth Low Energy. This link is budgeted within REQ-FD-02 in Section~\ref{sec:requirements}, which bounds how quickly a candidate fall event must reach the watch for validation as part of the overall delivery time.

\input{table4_requirements}

\textbf{Trial counts and thresholds.} Rate- and timing-based criteria (REQ-FD-02, REQ-FD-03, REQ-FD-04) use $N=20$ trials per category, since at this count each trial corresponds to a $5\%$ increment, matching the resolution of the stated miss-rate threshold; binary pass/fail requirements (REQ-FD-01, REQ-FD-05, REQ-FD-06) use $N=10$ repetitions, enough to catch an intermittent failure without requiring the statistical power a rate claim needs. Every numeric bound above is derived from a stated assumption or an existing figure in the table rather than asserted without reasoning, but none has yet been validated by measurement; REQ-FD-07's cost cap in particular rests on a rough component estimate rather than an actual quotation, and any revision made after testing will be recorded as a new dated logbook entry.

\section{Uncertainties and the PoC Plan}
\label{sec:uncertainties}

Three uncertainties are ranked below by how much of the design would collapse if the underlying assumption proved false, per the course guide's own rule for ranking. Each is written as a register entry; the single PoC plan attacks the highest-ranked uncertainty first.

\textbf{PoC Plan.} The highest-ranked uncertainty, R1, is attacked first, since it would most undermine the design if wrong. \emph{Question:} what false-alarm and miss rates occur in the system when combining independent fall signals from two separate sensors, and does a confirmed fall reach its destination in time, even over a poor connection? \emph{Measure:} (a) false-alarm and miss rates on held-out data from a labeled set of scripted falls and confusable activities (sitting heavily, lying down, stairs), held out by session and, where possible, by person; (b) detection-to-dashboard delivery time and success rate at strong and weak link strength, and the system's behavior when the link is lost. \emph{Pass criterion, fixed in advance:} the miss rate is at most 5\% (REQ-FD-03), the false-alarm rate is below 10\% (REQ-FD-04), and every confirmed fall is delivered within 25\,s (REQ-FD-02) in all link conditions tested.

\section{Assumptions, Dependencies, and the Logbook}
\label{sec:assumptions}

\textbf{Assumptions.} This design rests on several claims taken as true but not yet tested:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt, partopsep=0pt]
\item Falls performed by healthy volunteers under scripted, controlled conditions are assumed to produce IMU and barometric signatures representative of an actual elderly fall.
\item Bluetooth Low Energy connectivity between a wrist-worn watch and a waist-worn custom board is assumed to remain reliable at typical in-home separation distances.
\item The watch's processing budget (C4) is assumed sufficient to run the on-device fall classifier and validation logic within the real-time constraints implied by REQ-FD-02's 200\,ms board-to-watch sub-budget.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Dependencies.} This design also counts on conditions outside the project's own control:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt, partopsep=0pt]
\item The caregiver, family member, and care facility each have a working internet connection capable of receiving an MQTT-forwarded alert.
\item The wearer consistently wears and charges both the watch and the custom PCB; the system cannot function if either device is left uncharged or unworn.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{AI-Assisted Steps.} An AI assistant was used throughout drafting to help interrogate needs into measurable requirements, classify requirements by type, check scope and needs for internal consistency, and review wording against the course guide's rules. Every AI-suggested number, rationale, and requirement statement was reviewed by the author and checked against the guide's examples before inclusion.
